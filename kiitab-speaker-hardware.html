Assembly, step by step

Build from the base up. Each step adds its parts to the model above; "Show in 3D" jumps there. Steps 13 and 14 build the top section on the bench before it goes on in step 15.

    The skins: clear plastic with small holes, and the fabric on it

    Each of the three covers (top section, sleeve and bottom section) is a thin glossy shell of clear polycarbonate, 1.2 mm thick and full of small holes. The holes let the sound out and the clear plastic lets the light net shine through. The fabric is stretched over the shell, so from outside you see only fabric, while the shell keeps the shape crisp and the corners round.

    The holes. 1.0 mm round holes on a 1.8 mm staggered grid, about 28% open, enough for the sound to pass without changing it. The sleeve has holes all over, so the fabric looks the same everywhere; the top and bottom sections have holes only where sound or air must pass. Switch off "Fabric on the skins" beside the model to see them.

    How the fabric is held. Every edge of a shell turns inwards into a narrow lip with a groove all the way round. The fabric is pulled over the shell, wrapped round the edge and pressed into the groove with a soft rubber cord, the way an insect screen is fixed into a window. The cord holds it tight and even with no glue on the holes, and it pulls out to wash or change the fabric. Where two skins meet, the two folded edges leave a thin shadow line, so the seam looks deliberate.

    How the skins come off. The top section is held by four magnets and two locating pins. It carries the top board, which connects through spring pins, so no cable needs unplugging. Lift it off and the sleeve slides up and away. The bottom section is held by two screws underneath, so a child cannot pull the whole cover off.

    Child safety. Every magnet sits in a closed pocket, glued and covered, so it can never come loose: small strong magnets are dangerous if swallowed.

    The fabric. A tube sewn with its seam on a back corner, cut 2 to 3% smaller than the shell so it stretches tight. Linen, cotton or canvas chosen so sound and light pass through: hold it up to a lamp and you should see the light clearly. For the factory, a snap-in plastic clip ring can replace the rubber cord; it is faster to fit but harder to change at home.

    How a speaker that turns gets its power

    The wooden base stays still and the whole body turns on a ring bearing. The bottom motor sits inside the body, in one corner, and its small gear walks round a fixed gear ring on the base, carrying the speaker with it. A full turn takes about a minute and is nearly silent, because the motor driver runs it in its quiet mode.

    Because the body turns, no wire can go from the base to the body. Instead two copper power rings on the base carry 20 V, and two gold spring contacts under the body ride on them, like a cordless kettle on its stand. The base needs no board at all: just the USB-C socket, the power switch, the rings and a small magnet. A home sensor in the body feels that magnet, so the speaker always knows where the front is and can turn to face you.

    All the data stays inside the body. The remote and the app talk to the main board by Bluetooth and Wi-Fi, which work from any side.

    The top, and the top motor

    Under the wooden top is the top board: four microphones at the corners (they hear the name from any side), the voice chip that removes the speaker's own sound from what the microphones hear, the touch pads under the 3 mm wood, and the 24 lights of the ring.

    The top motor turns the add-on seat in the middle of the top. Magnets hold an add-on in place and two small contact rings power its lights while it turns, so a dome, minarets or the Kaaba's mataf can turn slowly while it recites, or stay still. This is my reading of "top motor"; see question 1 below.

    The same bottom half fits every shape. Base, turning plate, bottom motor, power board and bass speaker are shared by Classic, Mihrab, Front screen and Small display. Only the top half and the screen change.

    The Kiitab family: from the simplest to the most advanced

    Every model shares the same bottom half: base, turning plate, bottom motor, power board and bass speaker. Only the top half and the screen change, so each step up is a new top, not a new speaker.

    StepModelScreenWhat it adds
    1Plain (K1-P)NoneThis study: the light net and the top ring carry the character.
    2FaceOne flexible AMOLED on the frontShows the character's face, and turns with the body.
    3WrapFlexible AMOLED all the way roundThe character can be seen from any side.
    4CubeAMOLED fully covering all four sidesThe most advanced: the most power, heat and cost, so it comes last.
    NewSpeakerOne full AMOLED sideA modern speaker shape: speakers on the other sides for clear sound, operation buttons on top, and a strong base that does not turn, so it needs its own base instead of the shared turning base.

    AMOLED pixels light themselves, so no backlight is needed. Burn-in is a risk for a face that stays still for long, so the plan is to move and dim the face over time.

    Questions for the owner

    1. Top motor. I have it turning the add-on seat (a dome or the Kaaba's mataf turning slowly). Did you mean something else, such as opening a lid, tilting the top or lifting an add-on?
    2. Battery. This design runs from the wall only. A battery for carrying it room to room would sit in the bottom half, add about 250 g, and needs a charging circuit.
    3. The grille under the base. The design mockup shows a speaker grille under the base. With a turning body and a 13 mm base, the bass speaker fits better in the body, firing down so its sound comes out of the gap around the base. The underside then has only feet, the switch and the label.
    4. Where the brain runs (decided). The Rockchip RK3576 on the main board runs the brain and the speech recognition on its NPU, and keeps recitations for offline use. The ESP32-S3 handles the lights, touch and motors. This is the owner's choice for the NPU and lower power, and the RAM and storage still need confirming.
    5. Microphone switch. Should there be a hardware switch at the back that cuts power to the microphones, with a small red light, for families who want to be sure it is not listening?
